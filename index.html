01 / INTERNATIONAL FINANCE

Foreign Exchange & Interest Rate Parity

A worked case study converting USD, EUR, JPY, and GBP and evaluating whether a quoted forward rate matches covered interest rate parity.

Currency analysis Financial reasoning
Read the case study

This case study uses a hypothetical USD-based company with EUR, JPY, and GBP exposures. It shows how quotation direction affects conversions and how to compare a forward quote with covered interest rate parity.

Inputs

Currency Spot quotation One-year forward One-year rate
USD Home currency — 3%
EUR $1.2000 per EUR $1.2200 per EUR 2%
JPY ¥110.00 per USD ¥112.00 per USD 1%
GBP $1.3500 per GBP $1.3400 per GBP 4%

Spot conversions

  • $1,000,000 ÷ 1.2000 = €833,333.33.
  • ¥200,000,000 ÷ 110.00 = $1,818,181.82.
  • £500,000 × 1.3500 = $675,000.00.

Does the EUR forward quote satisfy parity?

For a quote in USD per EUR, the theoretical forward is:

F = S × (1 + rUSD) / (1 + rEUR)

F = 1.2000 × 1.03 ÷ 1.02 = $1.2117647 per EUR. The quoted $1.2200 differs, so parity does not hold exactly under these assumptions.

Implied EUR interest rate

Rearranging gives rEUR = (1.2000 × 1.03 ÷ 1.2200) − 1 = 1.3115%, or 1.31% rounded to two decimal places.

The calculations assume one-year effective rates and ignore transaction costs, bid–ask spreads, taxes, and borrowing constraints.

What this demonstrates

Consistent currency units, numerical analysis, and the ability to explain why a market quote differs from a theoretical benchmark.

02 / CAPITAL MARKETS

Understanding Treasury Auctions

A finance study guide explaining how bid-to-cover ratios, auction tails, and buyer participation help assess auction demand.

Market research Communication
Read the study guide

Treasury auctions provide a useful window into demand for government debt. Evaluating an auction requires comparing several indicators with recent auctions of the same maturity.

Three indicators to examine

Indicator Meaning Interpretation
Bid-to-cover Total bids divided by the amount offered A higher ratio can suggest stronger demand, relative to comparable auctions.
Tail or stop-through Auction yield compared with the when-issued yield just before the auction A higher auction yield is a tail; a lower auction yield is a stop-through.
Buyer participation Allocation among indirect bidders, direct bidders, and primary dealers Consider whether end-investor demand or dealer absorption drove the result.

A hypothetical comparison

Suppose Auction A has a 2.6 bid-to-cover ratio and stops through its when-issued yield by one basis point, while Auction B has a 2.3 ratio and tails by two basis points. If both are for the same maturity and otherwise comparable, A provides stronger evidence of demand.

One basis point equals 0.01 percentage point. These figures are illustrative, not actual auction results.

Why context matters

A 10-year auction and a 20-year auction have different buyer bases and typical demand patterns. Their raw ratios should not be treated as directly interchangeable. Compare each result with its own recent history, and consider supply size, market volatility, and changes in yields before drawing a conclusion.

What this demonstrates

Interpreting financial indicators, separating data from inference, and translating market terminology into clear business communication.
